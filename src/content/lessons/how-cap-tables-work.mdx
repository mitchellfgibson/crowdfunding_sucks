A **capitalization table** — "cap table" — is simply the list of who owns a
company and how much. Every share, option, and convertible instrument lives on
it. When you invest in a crowdfunding raise, you are buying a row on that table.
Understanding it is the difference between knowing what you own and guessing.

## The basic idea

Ownership is measured in **shares**, and your stake is your shares divided by the
**fully diluted** total — every share that exists *or could exist*. That last
part trips up most first-time investors.

> Example: a company has 9,000,000 shares and sells you shares worth 1% at
> today's count. If it has also promised 1,000,000 shares to employees as
> options, the *fully diluted* total is 10,000,000 — and your "1%" is really
> 0.9%. Always ask for the fully diluted number.

## What sits on a cap table

- **Founders' common stock** — usually the largest block early on.
- **Employee option pool** — shares set aside to hire people. Counts against you.
- **Preferred stock** — what most professional investors buy; it carries extra
  rights (see below).
- **Convertibles (SAFEs and notes)** — promises to issue shares *later*, often at
  a discount. They're not on the share count yet, but they will be.

## Why your slice shrinks: preferences and the pool

Two things quietly reduce what common shareholders (often including crowdfunding
investors) actually receive:

1. **Liquidation preferences.** Preferred investors frequently get their money
   back *first* in a sale. A "1x preference" means they take their original
   dollars off the top before common shareholders see anything.
2. **The option pool.** Expanding the pool to hire creates new shares — diluting
   everyone who already owns.

## What to check before you invest

- The **fully diluted** share count, not just shares outstanding.
- How much is already promised in **SAFEs / convertible notes** (future dilution).
- Whether you're buying **common or preferred**, and what rights come with it.
- The size of the **option pool** and whether it's expected to grow.

A clean, disclosed cap table is a good sign. A vague one — "you'll own about 1%"
with no fully diluted basis — is a reason to slow down.
