Most early-stage startups do not return capital to investors. That isn't cynicism
— it's the base rate of the asset class. The useful thing isn't to fear it; it's
to recognize the *patterns* of failure so you can weigh a specific deal against
them.

## The structural reasons

- **Overvaluation.** A raise priced far above what the business can grow into
  means even a decent outcome doesn't return much to investors who bought high.
- **No path to liquidity.** Even successful private companies may never be
  acquired or go public. Without an exit, your shares can't turn back into cash.
- **Relentless dilution.** Companies that raise round after round can
  [dilute](/lessons/dilution-explained/) early crowd investors down to a sliver.
- **Retail as late-stage capital.** Sometimes the crowd is raised from *after*
  professional investors have passed — meaning you're funding a company others
  with more information declined.

## The operational reasons

- **Ran out of money.** The most common cause: the company couldn't raise the
  *next* round and shut down.
- **First-time founders.** Many crowdfunding raises are led by founders without a
  prior exit. Not disqualifying — but a real risk factor.
- **The product never found a market.** Traction in a pitch deck is not the same
  as durable demand.

## The transparency reasons

- **No outcome reporting.** Platforms rarely publish how their past deals
  actually performed, so failures are quiet and easy to miss.
- **Pay-to-play promotion.** When marketing spend, not merit, drives which deals
  get attention, the loudest deal isn't the best one.

## How to use this

Run any deal you're considering against the list:

1. Is it **priced** for a realistic outcome, or for a fantasy one?
2. Is there a **plausible exit**, or are you locked in indefinitely?
3. How many **more rounds** will it need, and what does that do to your stake?
4. Is the company being **promoted** heavily — and by whom?

> You can't eliminate failure risk in early-stage investing. You *can* avoid the
> failures that were predictable from the structure of the deal.
